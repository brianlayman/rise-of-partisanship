The Rise ofPartisanshipRevisited

2:12 animation with original soundtrack, "Drifting Apart." The two instruments move apart in stereo as the parties do.

Each dot is a member of the House. Two members are connected when they voted the same way so often that a pair like them is more likely to share a party than not. In 1969, more than a third of those connections still ran between the parties. In most Congresses since 2003, fewer than one in a hundred have.

It has happened before. In the Gilded Age of the 1880s and 1890s, the parties were nearly as divided as they were in the 1990s. The bipartisan House of the mid-20th century was the exception.

How to read these graphs

  • Dots: each dot is a member of the House. Blue is Democrat, red is Republican, purple is a third-party or independent member.
  • Lines: a line joins two members who voted the same way unusually often, more often than that Congress's threshold. The threshold is the level of agreement above which a pair is more likely to share a party than not. Thicker lines mean more agreement.
  • Clusters are voting blocs. Members pulled into the space between the clusters are the ones who often vote with both parties.
  • Bigger dots have more connections.
  • Position doesn't measure ideology. Democrats are always placed on the left and Republicans on the right, for consistency. Relative positions (up, down, left, or right) and exact distances carry no meaning on their own.
  • Compare years by the percentage, not the picture. Each graph is scaled to fill the frame, so the width of the gap between the parties can't be compared from one Congress to another. The cross-party percentage can.
  • Small dots stranded at the edges are usually members who cast few votes: they arrived late, left early or died in office. It doesn't mean they were extreme.

Same Names. Same Parties?

Partly, yes. Both names have been on the ballot since the 1850s, and one thing has held steady: in every Congress since 1869, Democrats have voted to the left of Republicans on the role of government in the economy. But the coalitions behind the names have been rebuilt more than once, and what "left" and "right" meant shifted underneath them.

In 1869, the Republicans were the party of Lincoln, Reconstruction and a strong federal government, backed by Northern business and newly enfranchised Black voters in the South. The Democrats were the party of states' rights and the white South. The New Deal of the 1930s made Democrats the party of activist government, and Black voters began moving to them. Civil rights then divided the Democrats. From 1937 into the 1970s, Southern Democrats often voted with Republicans in a "conservative coalition," which is why the mid-century graphs are so tangled.

The map changed last. Southern presidential votes began shifting in 1948 and 1964, but Southern House seats followed slowly, and Republicans did not hold most of them until after the 1994 election. As conservative Southern Democrats gave way to Republicans, each party became more uniform, and the two clusters pulled apart. Same names, and in one way the same parties: the economic dividing line held. But the people and places behind them changed.

The ideology claim comes from DW-NOMINATE scores in the same Voteview data. Historians still debate how much of the Southern shift came from race and how much from economic growth, suburbs and religion. The data shows when the seats moved, not why.

Method. Following Andris, Lee, Hamilton, Martino, Gunning and Selden, "The Rise of Partisanship and Super-Cooperators in the U.S. House of Representatives" (PLOS ONE, 2015): agreement counts the roll calls on which two members both voted yea or both voted nay ("present" counts as nay, absences are dropped). Each Congress's threshold is where the same-party and cross-party agreement distributions cross, found as the agreement count above which same-party pairs most outnumber cross-party pairs. Third-party members (purple) count as cross-party with everyone. Dot size reflects number of connections; line thickness reflects agreement count. Layouts use a force-directed (ForceAtlas2-style) method, so position conveys clustering, not exact distance.

Data. Voteview House roll-call votes and member records, 41st through 119th Congress (1869–2026), the 119th through October 2026. Congresses before about 1947 recorded far fewer roll calls (as few as 72), so their graphs rest on less evidence, and the House had fewer than 435 seats before 1913. "The South" means the 11 former Confederate states. Opening seat counts are estimated from members voting in each Congress's first 10 roll calls and may differ by a seat or two from official tallies.
