The Rise ofPartisanship

96-second animation with original soundtrack, "Drifting Apart."

Each dot is a member of the House. Two members are connected when they voted the same way so often that a pair like them is more likely to share a party than not. In 1969, more than a third of those connections still ran between the parties. In most Congresses since 2003, fewer than one in a hundred have.

81st Congress

1949–1951

President
Harry S. Truman (D)
Speaker
Sam Rayburn (D)
House at opening
263 D · 172 R · 1 other · Democratic majority
Network
275 roll calls · threshold 155 agreements · 11.5% of connections cross party lines
  • 1948: Truman's upset win; Democrats gain about 75 House seats
  • Fair Deal agenda
  • Korean War begins, June 1950

Truman's surprise 1948 victory swept Democrats back into control after two years of Republican rule. The Democratic majority was itself divided: Southern Democrats often voted with Republicans as the "conservative coalition," blocking civil rights and much of Truman's Fair Deal. That cross-cutting alliance is why so many connections in this graph run between the two clusters.

Democrats kept the House but with a thinner margin after the 1950 midterms, fought amid the Korean War and the rise of Senator Joseph McCarthy. Anti-communism, war spending and price controls dominated the agenda. Cross-party agreement stayed high because the conservative coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats decided many close votes.

Eisenhower's coattails gave Republicans a narrow House majority and unified government for the first time since 1931. Eisenhower governed as a moderate, often needing Democratic votes to pass his program over objections from his own party's right wing. It was the last Republican House for 40 years.

Johnson's landslide over Barry Goldwater produced one of the largest Democratic majorities in history. This "Great Society" Congress passed the Voting Rights Act, Medicare, Medicaid, federal aid to education and immigration reform in a burst of lawmaking. Goldwater's 1964 wins in the Deep South hinted at the coming regional realignment.

Speaker

  • 1994 "Republican Revolution": GOP gains 54 seats

This is the Congress the Contract with America created. Republicans won the House for the first time in 40 years, and Gingrich brought nearly every Contract item to a floor vote within 100 days. He centralized power in the Speaker's office. Budget standoffs with Clinton led to two government shutdowns before the sides agreed on welfare reform. Many conservative Southern Democratic seats turned Republican, so each party became more uniform.

Bob Livingston, chosen to succeed Gingrich, withdrew over his own scandal, and Dennis Hastert became Speaker with one of the narrowest majorities in decades. Method. Following Andris, Lee, Hamilton, Martino, Gunning and Selden, "The Rise of Partisanship and Super-Cooperators in the U.S. House of Representatives" (PLOS ONE, 2015): agreement counts the roll calls on which two members both voted yea or both voted nay ("present" counts as nay, absences are dropped). Each Congress's threshold is where the same-party and cross-party agreement histograms cross. Dot size reflects number of connections; line thickness reflects agreement count. Layouts use a force-directed (ForceAtlas2-style) method, so position conveys clustering, not exact distance.

Data. Voteview House roll-call votes and member records, 81st through 119th Congress, the 119th through October 2026. Opening seat counts are estimated from members voting in each Congress's first 30 roll calls and may differ by a seat or two from official tallies.
